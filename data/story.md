## Story so far

The 2026 Ebola outbreak began in early May in the Democratic Republic of the Congo's Ituri Province. The earliest known symptom onset is April 24; authorities formally declared the outbreak on May 15. Laboratory analysis at the Institut National de Recherche Biomedicale identified the pathogen as **Bundibugyo virus (BDBV)**, one of four orthoebolavirus species that cause Ebola disease in humans. Unlike the more common Zaire ebolavirus, Bundibugyo currently has no approved vaccine or specific therapeutic.

The World Health Organization declared the outbreak a [Public Health Emergency of International Concern](https://www.who.int/news/item/17-05-2026-epidemic-of-ebola-disease-in-the-democratic-republic-of-the-congo-and-uganda-determined-a-public-health-emergency-of-international-concern) on May 17. Africa CDC declared a Public Health Emergency of Continental Security on May 18. Within the first ten days, cases spread across 12 health zones in three DRC provinces (Ituri, Nord-Kivu, Sud-Kivu) plus imported and locally-transmitted cases in Kampala, Uganda.

WHO said on August 10 that transmission probably began in **February**, three months before the declaration. Early cases were recorded as malaria and typhoid, and the first laboratory tests looked for Zaire ebolavirus rather than Bundibugyo. Mohamed Janabi, WHO's regional director for Africa, described the response as running behind the virus. That head start is the mechanism behind most of what follows.

The response is operating under acute constraints: armed conflict in the affected zones, community distrust of containment measures (multiple Ebola treatment centers have been attacked, with patients escaping), high population mobility, and the absence of a Bundibugyo-specific vaccine. Healthcare-associated infections in Uganda raise concerns about undetected transmission chains in urban centers.

At day 100, on August 24, WHO published a [statement](https://www.afro.who.int/countries/democratic-republic-of-congo/news/over-5200-cases-recorded-democratic-republic-congocrosses100-days-ebola-outbreak-declaration) that sets the frame for everything since. It calls this the DRC's fastest-growing Ebola outbreak, averaging around 90 confirmed cases a day over three months, faster than the equivalent stretch of West Africa 2014-16 or Kivu 2018-20. It also lists what the response built in the same window. Testing went from one site to 19 labs and 3,000 samples a day. Treatment capacity went from under 10 beds to more than 1,300. Contact follow-up went from 9 percent in the first week to 84 percent by mid-August. Both halves are true at once, and that is the thing to hold onto. The response scaled by two orders of magnitude and the curve did not bend. The number that explains why sits in the mortality line: community deaths, the ones happening outside a treatment centre, run at about 60 percent of the roughly 260 deaths recorded each week. A death outside a treatment centre is a case the system never got its hands on. You can build 1,300 beds and still lose, because beds only work on people who reach them. WHO's stated targets from here are contact tracing at 95 percent and capacity at 3,000 beds.

## Latest update
**September 8.** The series reads **September 7: 6,707 confirmed cases, 3,228 deaths, 1,582 recovered**, and 6,823 cumulative including suspected cases. DRC alone stands at 6,686 confirmed and 3,226 deaths, a crude fatality rate of 48.3 percent. Active cases, meaning confirmed minus recovered minus confirmed deaths, sit at 1,897. September 8 is a gap. SitRep 116 has not published.

Two sources agree on those figures exactly. [SitRep 115](https://insp.cd/sitrep-n115-mvebdb-07-09-2026/) from INSP, and [WHO's Weekly External Situation Report 17](https://www.afro.who.int/countries/democratic-republic-of-congo/publication/ebola-bundibugyo-virus-disease-outbreak-0), data as of September 6. WHO also reconciles from the other end. It reports 586 confirmed cases and 276 deaths added since its previous weekly report, and the tracker's August 31 row carries DRC at 6,100 and 2,950. The arithmetic closes on both.

Nord-Kivu tied Ituri on new cases. Thirty-nine each, 47.6 percent of the day apiece. No province has matched Ituri's daily count before in this outbreak. Ituri still holds 80.2 percent of the cumulative, so the gap between what a province contributes today and what it holds in total is now wide open, and it is pointing at Nord-Kivu.

WHO's twenty-one-day windows say the same thing at a longer scale. Nationally, confirmed cases fell 5.3 percent. Inside that, Ituri fell 18.3 percent and Nord-Kivu rose 47.4 percent. A national decline made of a large decrease and a large increase is not a decline. It is a handoff.

The lab number needs care this time. Nord-Kivu's positivity dropped from 32.3 percent to 22.9 percent while its case count went up. That looks like improvement and it is, but not the kind that means less virus. Samples analysed went from 96 to 170, a 77 percent jump. When you widen the net, positivity falls because the denominator grew. What moved is testing capacity, not transmission.

The mortality line is where this report gets hard. Weekly confirmed deaths rose slightly, to 276. All of the increase was community deaths, meaning deaths outside a treatment centre, which went from 171 to 207. Deaths inside treatment facilities fell from 99 to 69. So the community share of confirmed deaths ran 56.6 percent, then 63.3, then 75.0 across three weeks. That is the highest the outbreak has recorded.

Hold that against the beds. Nord-Kivu ran at 123.2 percent occupancy, 262 patients for 220, oversubscribed for a seventh straight day. Ituri ran at 49.5 percent, 484 patients for 978. There is spare capacity in this response and it is in the wrong province. But the 75 percent number says something harder than a distribution problem. Three quarters of the people dying of this never reach a bed at all. WHO's own reading is that bed capacity alone is now insufficient and the binding constraint is the pathway to care: detection, notification, referral, transport, and whether a family trusts the system enough to use it.

On September 4 the government launched a revised 180-day response plan with WHO, the UN system and Africa CDC. It is costed at 1.3 billion dollars. The IHR Emergency Committee met on August 18 and held that the outbreak remains far from controlled and remains a Public Health Emergency of International Concern.

The smaller numbers held. Contact follow-up was 85.3 percent, down from 85.7 and a fifth consecutive day above the operational threshold. All 215 validated suspects were investigated within the day, and 99 were transferred to a treatment centre. Vaccination in Bas-Uélé is still stopped on the Buta stock-out. Ituri validated a plan to start front-line vaccination in Bunia and Mongbwalu with MSF.
