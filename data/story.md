## Story so far

The 2026 Ebola outbreak began in early May in the Democratic Republic of the Congo's Ituri Province. The earliest known symptom onset is April 24; authorities formally declared the outbreak on May 15. Laboratory analysis at the Institut National de Recherche Biomedicale identified the pathogen as **Bundibugyo virus (BDBV)**, one of four orthoebolavirus species that cause Ebola disease in humans. Unlike the more common Zaire ebolavirus, Bundibugyo currently has no approved vaccine or specific therapeutic.

The World Health Organization declared the outbreak a [Public Health Emergency of International Concern](https://www.who.int/news/item/17-05-2026-epidemic-of-ebola-disease-in-the-democratic-republic-of-the-congo-and-uganda-determined-a-public-health-emergency-of-international-concern) on May 17. Africa CDC declared a Public Health Emergency of Continental Security on May 18. Within the first ten days, cases spread across 12 health zones in three DRC provinces (Ituri, Nord-Kivu, Sud-Kivu) plus imported and locally-transmitted cases in Kampala, Uganda.

The response is operating under acute constraints: armed conflict in the affected zones, community distrust of containment measures (multiple Ebola treatment centers have been attacked, with patients escaping), high population mobility, and the absence of a Bundibugyo-specific vaccine. Healthcare-associated infections in Uganda raise concerns about undetected transmission chains in urban centers.

## Latest update

**July 28.** Two things happened at once, pointing opposite directions. Uganda declared its outbreak over. DRC posted its worst weekly reconciliation yet.

Start with Uganda. On July 28 the Ministry of Health said the country is Ebola-free. Final count: 20 cases, 18 recovered, two deaths. The last patient was discharged on July 16, so the standard 42-day countdown had another two and a half weeks to run. Uganda declared early anyway, and the reasoning is worth looking at, because it is a methodological argument and not a political one.

The 42-day rule is two incubation periods. You wait that long because you cannot see the transmission chains directly, so you let the clock stand in for certainty. Uganda's claim is that it could see the chains. Every confirmed case linked back to a known source, every contact traced and followed to the end, surveillance across the country turning up nothing new. When you have reconstructed the whole structure, the ministry argued, the clock is measuring something you already know. So they stopped it.

That is a defensible position and also a bet. The countdown exists for the cases you did not catch, and you cannot prove that set is empty by looking harder. Baryomunsi said as much in the same breath: Ebola stays a threat as long as DRC is still burning, and the clock resets to zero if a new case crosses the border. Uganda's "over" is conditional on a fence it does not control.

Across that fence, the books finally caught up. WHO published two situation reports at once, Sitrep 10 and Sitrep 11, closing a reconciliation gap that had run since July 12. [Sitrep 11](https://www.afro.who.int/countries/democratic-republic-of-congo/publication/ebola-bundibugyo-virus-disease-outbreak-3), data as of July 26, puts DRC at **3,262 confirmed cases, 1,437 deaths, and 583 recovered**. With Uganda and France that is **3,283 confirmed and 1,439 deaths** globally. WHO calls it the largest Bundibugyo virus outbreak ever recorded.

Here is the part that inverts the usual pattern. For weeks the running wire count has sat above the last WHO reconciliation, and every Sitrep has closed the gap by revising upward toward it. This time the Sitrep landed above the running count. BNO's July 26 graphic had DRC at 3,200. WHO reconciled it to 3,262, sixty-two cases higher, on the same day. The reconciliation is no longer catching the count up. It is finding cases the count never reached.

The fatality ratio holds near **44 percent**. More than four in ten confirmed infections end in a recorded death, and that is before Tedros's standing caveat that insecurity keeps the true case count unknown and probably far higher. One country closed its chapter this week by arguing it could see everything. The other keeps discovering how much it cannot.
