## Story so far

The 2026 Ebola outbreak began in early May in the Democratic Republic of the Congo's Ituri Province. The earliest known symptom onset is April 24; authorities formally declared the outbreak on May 15. Laboratory analysis at the Institut National de Recherche Biomedicale identified the pathogen as **Bundibugyo virus (BDBV)**, one of four orthoebolavirus species that cause Ebola disease in humans. Unlike the more common Zaire ebolavirus, Bundibugyo currently has no approved vaccine or specific therapeutic.

The World Health Organization declared the outbreak a [Public Health Emergency of International Concern](https://www.who.int/news/item/17-05-2026-epidemic-of-ebola-disease-in-the-democratic-republic-of-the-congo-and-uganda-determined-a-public-health-emergency-of-international-concern) on May 17. Africa CDC declared a Public Health Emergency of Continental Security on May 18. Within the first ten days, cases spread across 12 health zones in three DRC provinces (Ituri, Nord-Kivu, Sud-Kivu) plus imported and locally-transmitted cases in Kampala, Uganda.

WHO said on August 10 that transmission probably began in **February**, three months before the declaration. Early cases were recorded as malaria and typhoid, and the first laboratory tests looked for Zaire ebolavirus rather than Bundibugyo. Mohamed Janabi, WHO's regional director for Africa, described the response as running behind the virus. That head start is the mechanism behind most of what follows.

The response is operating under acute constraints: armed conflict in the affected zones, community distrust of containment measures (multiple Ebola treatment centers have been attacked, with patients escaping), high population mobility, and the absence of a Bundibugyo-specific vaccine. Healthcare-associated infections in Uganda raise concerns about undetected transmission chains in urban centers.

At day 100, on August 24, WHO published a [statement](https://www.afro.who.int/countries/democratic-republic-of-congo/news/over-5200-cases-recorded-democratic-republic-congocrosses100-days-ebola-outbreak-declaration) that sets the frame for everything since. It calls this the DRC's fastest-growing Ebola outbreak, averaging around 90 confirmed cases a day over three months, faster than the equivalent stretch of West Africa 2014-16 or Kivu 2018-20. It also lists what the response built in the same window. Testing went from one site to 19 labs and 3,000 samples a day. Treatment capacity went from under 10 beds to more than 1,300. Contact follow-up went from 9 percent in the first week to 84 percent by mid-August. Both halves are true at once, and that is the thing to hold onto. The response scaled by two orders of magnitude and the curve did not bend. The number that explains why sits in the mortality line: community deaths, the ones happening outside a treatment centre, run at about 60 percent of the roughly 260 deaths recorded each week. A death outside a treatment centre is a case the system never got its hands on. You can build 1,300 beds and still lose, because beds only work on people who reach them. WHO's stated targets from here are contact tracing at 95 percent and capacity at 3,000 beds.

## Latest update
**August 30.** No new figure landed today. INSP has not posted SitRep 107, so the series still runs through **August 29: 5,966 confirmed cases, 2,864 deaths, 1,346 recovered**. The global cumulative total, suspected plus confirmed, stands at 6,082. DRC alone is at 5,945 confirmed and 2,862 deaths, a case fatality ratio of 48.1 percent.

What did land is the paperwork from the August 18 Emergency Committee, and it is the sharpest thing WHO has published about this outbreak. The [meeting report](https://www.who.int/news/item/28-08-2026-second-meeting-of-the-ihr-emergency-committee-on-the-epidemic-of-ebola-bundibugyo-virus-disease-in-the-democratic-republic-of-the-congo---meeting-report) came out on the 28th, and the [updated temporary recommendations](https://www.who.int/news/item/24-08-2026-second-meeting-of-the-ihr-emergency-committee-on-the-epidemic-of-ebola-bundibugyo-virus-disease-in-the-democratic-republic-of-the-congo-temporary-recommendations) went to States Parties on the 24th. The Director-General kept the emergency in force and ruled that it does not meet the criteria for a pandemic emergency. [DON616](https://www.who.int/emergencies/disease-outbreak-news/item/2026-DON616), published the same day as the meeting report, carries 5,815 confirmed and 2,788 deaths as of August 26, which matches this tracker's August 27 row exactly.

The headline finding is that the committee thinks the real epidemic is three to four times bigger than the surveillance data. You can see how they got there. Fit the growth to confirmed cases and you get a doubling time of 52 days and an Rt of 1.24. Fit it to deaths and you get 21 days and an Rt of 1.55. The committee takes the mortality version, because a death gets ascertained more reliably than a case. So the gap between those two numbers is not a modelling disagreement. It is a measurement of how much the case-finding system is missing.

The contact-tracing figure works the same way, and this is the part worth sitting with. Follow-up of listed contacts runs around 80 percent, which reads like a system doing its job. But the average case has only 13 listed contacts, far below what previous viral hemorrhagic fever outbreaks recorded. The result is that about 20 percent of cases are found through contact follow-up at all, and only 40 percent ever get linked to a chain of transmission. The percentage looks healthy because the list is short. Contact tracing at 80 percent of the wrong denominator is not contact tracing.

Capacity is still short of the target on every line. Twenty-one labs. Roughly 1,000 treatment beds against an estimated 3,000 needed. WHO has 274 staff deployed against a target of 455. Ninety-four partners are reporting engagement, concentrated in Ituri, North Kivu and South Kivu, which leaves the rest of the country thin. The airports at Goma and Bukavu are closed, and the committee links that closure directly to recurrent stock-outs of supplies.

Then the collateral line, which the response has been carrying since May and which rarely makes the case counts. Use of health services has fallen by more than 40 percent in some areas with transmission. Malaria, cholera and trauma cases have not fallen with it. Maternal deaths in Ituri have doubled since May and now run above six a week.

The new recommendations reach further into daily life than the May set did. There is a section that did not exist before, covering social measures, mass gatherings and domestic mobility: postpone mass gatherings in areas with transmission, reduce crowding in bars and nightclubs, limit motorbikes to one passenger, open schools under specific measures, run 24/7 health check points on the roads connecting infected areas to at-risk ones, and put BVD surveillance on the inland waterway vessels that run to Kinshasa. That last one is the tell. Somebody is modelling the river as a transmission route to a city of seventeen million.

Uganda's standing changed on paper. The committee commends Uganda for control, with no new case since July 16, and the recommendations move it out of the community-transmission tier into the group of States Parties bordering one, alongside Angola, Burundi, Central African Republic, Republic of Congo, Rwanda, South Sudan, Tanzania and Zambia. That tier gets readiness and border-health obligations rather than response obligations. Readiness scores in that group are uneven: Rwanda at 94 percent, Zambia 70, South Sudan 65, Angola 64, Tanzania 53, Republic of Congo 50, Central African Republic 38, with no data for Burundi or Kenya.

One committee member dissented, and WHO recorded it in an annex. The objection is not about the science. It is that the committee's statement, by getting into operational management, resource mobilization, political engagement and humanitarian access, went past what Articles 12, 17, 48 and 49 give it authority to say. Worth noting because the rest of the statement is an unusually direct call for an operational reset, and one member thought that was not the committee's call to make.
