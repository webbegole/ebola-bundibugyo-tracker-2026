## Story so far

The 2026 Ebola outbreak began in early May in the Democratic Republic of the Congo's Ituri Province. The earliest known symptom onset is April 24; authorities formally declared the outbreak on May 15. Laboratory analysis at the Institut National de Recherche Biomedicale identified the pathogen as **Bundibugyo virus (BDBV)**, one of four orthoebolavirus species that cause Ebola disease in humans. Unlike the more common Zaire ebolavirus, Bundibugyo currently has no approved vaccine or specific therapeutic.

The World Health Organization declared the outbreak a [Public Health Emergency of International Concern](https://www.who.int/news/item/17-05-2026-epidemic-of-ebola-disease-in-the-democratic-republic-of-the-congo-and-uganda-determined-a-public-health-emergency-of-international-concern) on May 17. Africa CDC declared a Public Health Emergency of Continental Security on May 18. Within the first ten days, cases spread across 12 health zones in three DRC provinces (Ituri, Nord-Kivu, Sud-Kivu) plus imported and locally-transmitted cases in Kampala, Uganda.

WHO said on August 10 that transmission probably began in **February**, three months before the declaration. Early cases were recorded as malaria and typhoid, and the first laboratory tests looked for Zaire ebolavirus rather than Bundibugyo. Mohamed Janabi, WHO's regional director for Africa, described the response as running behind the virus. That head start is the mechanism behind most of what follows.

The response is operating under acute constraints: armed conflict in the affected zones, community distrust of containment measures (multiple Ebola treatment centers have been attacked, with patients escaping), high population mobility, and the absence of a Bundibugyo-specific vaccine. Healthcare-associated infections in Uganda raise concerns about undetected transmission chains in urban centers.

At day 100, on August 24, WHO published a [statement](https://www.afro.who.int/countries/democratic-republic-of-congo/news/over-5200-cases-recorded-democratic-republic-congocrosses100-days-ebola-outbreak-declaration) that sets the frame for everything since. It calls this the DRC's fastest-growing Ebola outbreak, averaging around 90 confirmed cases a day over three months, faster than the equivalent stretch of West Africa 2014-16 or Kivu 2018-20. It also lists what the response built in the same window. Testing went from one site to 19 labs and 3,000 samples a day. Treatment capacity went from under 10 beds to more than 1,300. Contact follow-up went from 9 percent in the first week to 84 percent by mid-August. Both halves are true at once, and that is the thing to hold onto. The response scaled by two orders of magnitude and the curve did not bend. The number that explains why sits in the mortality line: community deaths, the ones happening outside a treatment centre, run at about 60 percent of the roughly 260 deaths recorded each week. A death outside a treatment centre is a case the system never got its hands on. You can build 1,300 beds and still lose, because beds only work on people who reach them. WHO's stated targets from here are contact tracing at 95 percent and capacity at 3,000 beds.

## Latest update

**September 15.** The DRC recorded **58 new confirmed cases** on September 13, the smallest single-day count in weeks, against 87 the day before and 91 the day before that. Nord-Kivu took 12 of them. Two days earlier Nord-Kivu had taken 50, more than half the national day.

The laboratory line is what makes that number worth reading. Nord-Kivu ran **7.3 percent positivity on 164 samples**, down from 23.5 percent on 157 samples on September 11. Ituri ran 20.9 percent on 211 samples and supplied 44 of the 58 cases. Same testing throughput in Nord-Kivu, a third of the positive results. That is the shape of a province where the sampled population is getting less infected, not a province that tested less.

One day is not a trend, and the day has a hole in it. Contact follow-up fell to **78.6 percent** (21,935 of 27,905) from 88.0 percent, below the 85 percent operational threshold, and [INSP](https://insp.cd/sitrep-n122-mvebdb-13-09-2026/) attributes the drop to poor reporting completeness in Nord-Kivu and Haut-Uélé. A low case count on a day the tracing system reported worse is not the same as a low case count on a day it reported better. Some of the missing cases may simply be missing.

Inflow 58, outflow 49, so derived active cases rose by 9 to 2,022. The narrowest gap between the two since active crossed 2,000.

U.N. officials made the same point from Geneva on September 15 with more caution than the daily numbers invite. Julien Harneis, the U.N. special Ebola coordinator, said there are areas of progress and other areas where cases continue to grow. WHO's Olivier le Polain said it is "too early to confidently say we have passed a peak" and that full control will take "many, many months" ([Reuters, 15 September](https://1027wbow.com/2026/09/15/un-says-ebola-epidemic-still-huge-and-growing/)). Congo's health minister Samuel Roger Kamba had gone further on Saturday, citing a mid-August peak, daily cases down from roughly 120 to roughly 80, and 10 of 62 affected health zones with no new case for more than 22 days.

Nord-Kivu is still where this is decided. It holds 1,269 of its confirmed cases as of September 12, and 555 of those were reported in the trailing 21 days. Its treatment structures ran **145.6 percent occupancy** on September 13 and 45 percent of hospitalised patients are being managed outside a treatment centre. The U.N. called the situation there alarming on the same day the province reported 12 cases. Both are descriptions of the same place.

Cumulative: **7,258 confirmed cases and 3,510 deaths** in the DRC across seven provinces, a case fatality rate of 48.4 percent, 62 of 167 health zones affected.

CDC has quietly closed Uganda's file. The Level 2 travel notice that carried both countries is now titled for the Democratic Republic of the Congo alone, last reviewed August 28, the day after WHO and Africa CDC declared the Uganda outbreak over. Uganda survives in the notice only as an entry-screening requirement for travellers arriving in the United States.

WHO's newest weekly reconciliation is still [Situation Report 17](https://www.afro.who.int/health-topics/ebola-disease/outbreak-drc-26), data as of September 6. Nine days on a weekly cadence. The last eight bars on every chart are drawn as provisional.
