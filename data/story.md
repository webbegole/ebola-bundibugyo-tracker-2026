## Story so far

The 2026 Ebola outbreak began in early May in the Democratic Republic of the Congo's Ituri Province. The earliest known symptom onset is April 24; authorities formally declared the outbreak on May 15. Laboratory analysis at the Institut National de Recherche Biomedicale identified the pathogen as **Bundibugyo virus (BDBV)**, one of four orthoebolavirus species that cause Ebola disease in humans. Unlike the more common Zaire ebolavirus, Bundibugyo currently has no approved vaccine or specific therapeutic.

The World Health Organization declared the outbreak a [Public Health Emergency of International Concern](https://www.who.int/news/item/17-05-2026-epidemic-of-ebola-disease-in-the-democratic-republic-of-the-congo-and-uganda-determined-a-public-health-emergency-of-international-concern) on May 17. Africa CDC declared a Public Health Emergency of Continental Security on May 18. Within the first ten days, cases spread across 12 health zones in three DRC provinces (Ituri, Nord-Kivu, Sud-Kivu) plus imported and locally-transmitted cases in Kampala, Uganda.

WHO said on August 10 that transmission probably began in **February**, three months before the declaration. Early cases were recorded as malaria and typhoid, and the first laboratory tests looked for Zaire ebolavirus rather than Bundibugyo. Mohamed Janabi, WHO's regional director for Africa, described the response as running behind the virus. That head start is the mechanism behind most of what follows.

The response is operating under acute constraints: armed conflict in the affected zones, community distrust of containment measures (multiple Ebola treatment centers have been attacked, with patients escaping), high population mobility, and the absence of a Bundibugyo-specific vaccine. Healthcare-associated infections in Uganda raise concerns about undetected transmission chains in urban centers.

At day 100, on August 24, WHO published a [statement](https://www.afro.who.int/countries/democratic-republic-of-congo/news/over-5200-cases-recorded-democratic-republic-congocrosses100-days-ebola-outbreak-declaration) that sets the frame for everything since. It calls this the DRC's fastest-growing Ebola outbreak, averaging around 90 confirmed cases a day over three months, faster than the equivalent stretch of West Africa 2014-16 or Kivu 2018-20. It also lists what the response built in the same window. Testing went from one site to 19 labs and 3,000 samples a day. Treatment capacity went from under 10 beds to more than 1,300. Contact follow-up went from 9 percent in the first week to 84 percent by mid-August. Both halves are true at once, and that is the thing to hold onto. The response scaled by two orders of magnitude and the curve did not bend. The number that explains why sits in the mortality line: community deaths, the ones happening outside a treatment centre, run at about 60 percent of the roughly 260 deaths recorded each week. A death outside a treatment centre is a case the system never got its hands on. You can build 1,300 beds and still lose, because beds only work on people who reach them. WHO's stated targets from here are contact tracing at 95 percent and capacity at 3,000 beds.

## Latest update
**September 2.** No new figure landed. INSP had not published SitRep 110 by the close of this run, so the series still reads **September 1: 6,207 confirmed cases, 3,009 deaths, 1,428 recovered**, and 6,323 cumulative including suspected cases. DRC alone stands at 6,186 confirmed and 3,007 deaths.

What did land is the first published scorecard of the response. CDC's [Notes from the Field](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/75/wr/mm7535e1.htm), out 1 September, takes the five operational domains that ended previous Ebola outbreaks, puts this response's numbers beside the targets, and reports the result as of 21 August. Contact tracing identifies 10.6 contacts per confirmed case against a target of 20 or more. Completeness of contact follow-up runs 82 percent against 95. Of new confirmed cases, 15 to 20 percent were already on a contact list, where the target is above 90. Laboratory testing reaches 72 percent of validated alerts against a target above 90, and 24 percent of those tests come back positive, where the target is zero. Fifty-nine percent of confirmed deaths happen outside a treatment unit, target zero. Forty-nine percent of affected health zones have at least one safe and dignified burial team, where the target is all of them.

One indicator passes. National treatment-unit bed occupancy is 64 percent against a target below 80. The report qualifies it in the same breath: occupancy runs as high as 140 percent in individual health zones. Two indicators have no current data at all, and one of the missing ones is the share of confirmed cases isolated within 24 hours. CDC's conclusion puts the gaps and the numbers together and calls the result "uncontrolled expansion of the outbreak."

Read the indicators as a chain and that phrase stops being a verdict and becomes a mechanism. Test positivity at 24 percent means the net is being cast over people who already very likely have it, which is what testing looks like when you are finding cases late rather than early. 10.6 contacts per case against an expected 20 means about half the exposure never gets written down. Four in five new confirmed cases arriving from outside the contact lists means the map is missing most of the transmission it is supposed to describe. And 59 percent of deaths outside a treatment unit means the system meets most of its cases after they are already dead. Same failure, four places along the same chain: the response is finding this outbreak downstream of transmission rather than upstream of it.

One detail in the report matters to how this tracker works. CDC says it abstracts its indicator data from DRC's publicly available daily situation reports on insp.cd, which is the same primary source this tracker has cited since 18 August. The report's own case count, 5,458 confirmed and 2,606 deaths in DRC as of 21 August, matches this tracker's 22 August row exactly.

WHO put a price on the next six months. At a [High-Level Member States briefing on 1 September](https://www.who.int/news-room/speeches/item/who-director-general-s-opening-remarks-at-the-high-level-member-states-briefing-on-the-ebola-response-in-the-democratic-republic-of-the-congo--1-september-2026), Tedros gave the government-led plan a cost of **US$ 1.3 billion** and said three vaccines and three therapeutics are now in trials. He also named, in plain language, what the CDC indicators measure: "many deaths are among people who were not on a list of known contacts. That means there are chains of transmission we don't know about." He repeated it to reporters in Geneva the next day. Meanwhile schools in the worst-hit areas reopened this week, after authorities concluded that remote learning would cost students more than the virus would.
