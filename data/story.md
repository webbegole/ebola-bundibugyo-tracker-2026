## Story so far

The 2026 Ebola outbreak began in early May in the Democratic Republic of the Congo's Ituri Province. The earliest known symptom onset is April 24; authorities formally declared the outbreak on May 15. Laboratory analysis at the Institut National de Recherche Biomedicale identified the pathogen as **Bundibugyo virus (BDBV)**, one of four orthoebolavirus species that cause Ebola disease in humans. Unlike the more common Zaire ebolavirus, Bundibugyo currently has no approved vaccine or specific therapeutic.

The World Health Organization declared the outbreak a [Public Health Emergency of International Concern](https://www.who.int/news/item/17-05-2026-epidemic-of-ebola-disease-in-the-democratic-republic-of-the-congo-and-uganda-determined-a-public-health-emergency-of-international-concern) on May 17. Africa CDC declared a Public Health Emergency of Continental Security on May 18. Within the first ten days, cases spread across 12 health zones in three DRC provinces (Ituri, Nord-Kivu, Sud-Kivu) plus imported and locally-transmitted cases in Kampala, Uganda.

WHO said on August 10 that transmission probably began in **February**, three months before the declaration. Early cases were recorded as malaria and typhoid, and the first laboratory tests looked for Zaire ebolavirus rather than Bundibugyo. Mohamed Janabi, WHO's regional director for Africa, described the response as running behind the virus. That head start is the mechanism behind most of what follows.

The response is operating under acute constraints: armed conflict in the affected zones, community distrust of containment measures (multiple Ebola treatment centers have been attacked, with patients escaping), high population mobility, and the absence of a Bundibugyo-specific vaccine. Healthcare-associated infections in Uganda raise concerns about undetected transmission chains in urban centers.

At day 100, on August 24, WHO published a [statement](https://www.afro.who.int/countries/democratic-republic-of-congo/news/over-5200-cases-recorded-democratic-republic-congocrosses100-days-ebola-outbreak-declaration) that sets the frame for everything since. It calls this the DRC's fastest-growing Ebola outbreak, averaging around 90 confirmed cases a day over three months, faster than the equivalent stretch of West Africa 2014-16 or Kivu 2018-20. It also lists what the response built in the same window. Testing went from one site to 19 labs and 3,000 samples a day. Treatment capacity went from under 10 beds to more than 1,300. Contact follow-up went from 9 percent in the first week to 84 percent by mid-August. Both halves are true at once, and that is the thing to hold onto. The response scaled by two orders of magnitude and the curve did not bend. The number that explains why sits in the mortality line: community deaths, the ones happening outside a treatment centre, run at about 60 percent of the roughly 260 deaths recorded each week. A death outside a treatment centre is a case the system never got its hands on. You can build 1,300 beds and still lose, because beds only work on people who reach them. WHO's stated targets from here are contact tracing at 95 percent and capacity at 3,000 beds.

## Latest update

**September 17.** The DRC's INSP published two situation reports at once, and the two-day freeze in national figures is over. SitRep 123 carries data through September 14. SitRep 124 carries data through September 15. Neither appeared in the insp.cd feed or the site's own SitRep listing; both were found by probing the PDF path directly, which is the third time this month the publication channel has hidden a report that was already sitting on the server.

The series now ends on **September 16**, carrying data through September 15: **7,404 confirmed cases and 3,577 deaths** in the DRC, 7,425 confirmed and 3,579 deaths across the DRC, Uganda and France, 1,795 recovered, and 2,051 derived active cases. September 17 is held as a gap. SitRep 125 had not published at run time and no other source carries an as-of-September-16 figure.

Two things in those reports are worth naming separately from the totals.

The first is the daily case count. September 15 brought 59 new confirmed cases, the lowest single-day figure since August 25 and the first reading under 80 in a run of 80, 91, 87, 87, 99 and 87. One day is one day. But the composition moved with it. Haut-Uélé recorded its first zero-case day since it entered the series, on 13 samples that all came back negative. Ituri took 35 of the 59 and Nord-Kivu 21.

The second is a bed count, and it is the more mechanical signal. Nord-Kivu treatment capacity had been oversubscribed for twelve consecutive days, peaking at 152.6 percent on September 14. On September 15 it reads 94.7 percent. The number that explains the drop is not the patient count, which went up, from 348 to 373. It is that INSP now records 42.1 percent of Nord-Kivu inpatients as managed outside a normed structure, and still reports no bed available at Butembo or at Katwa. So the ratio fell because the denominator grew, not because fewer people needed a bed. That is capacity catching up to demand, which is the thing you want, and it is not the same as demand falling.

Whether the curve has actually turned is now an open disagreement between the people running the response. Congo's health minister, Samuel Roger Kamba, said on September 12 that there were "encouraging signs" since "the peak reached in mid-August" and that transmission "has been gradually slowing down". Africa CDC does not accept the reading. Its Emergency Consultative Group said the data "could not confirm that the peak of the outbreak has been reached, and the transmission is not yet consistently controlled across all affected areas". Director-General Jean Kaseya said the Congolese assessment rests on incomplete data and that he would need a sustained decline before calling a peak. "We saw a decrease in some areas, but we say the situation is still serious" ([Reuters, 17 September](https://1027wbow.com/2026/09/17/ebola-outbreak-in-congo-is-still-serious-african-health-body-says/)).

The argument is about what counts as evidence, not about what the numbers say. Both sides are looking at the same falling Ituri line and the same rising Nord-Kivu line. A peak claim needs the second to stop offsetting the first, and it needs enough days to rule out reporting noise. Neither condition is met yet in this series.

Underneath all of it the geography keeps widening. Bas-Uélé notified two confirmed cases in the Viadana health zone on September 15, its first multi-case day, taking the province from 5 to 7, and it returned the highest laboratory positivity of any province in that report at 33.3 percent on 6 samples. Sud Ubangi, the seventh province and the one 1,500 km from the epicentre, drew its first suspect samples and acquired 300 doses of ERVEBO. The affected count holds at 62 of 167 health zones.
